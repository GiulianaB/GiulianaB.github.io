## Abstract

The California Undercurrent's signature does not arrive at offshore waters of the NE Pacific shelf-slope system steadily — it pulses, intermittently strengthening and weakening on timescales of days to weeks. This intermittent signature is documented using 38 repeat glider transects ("Southern Line," off Vancouver Island, 2024–2026). The persistent cross-shore thermohaline gradient reflects mixing between the poleward California Undercurrent (CUC), carrying warm, saline Pacific Equatorial Water, and the colder Pacific Subarctic Upper Water of the Gulf of Alaska. On the σ₀ = 26.6 kg m⁻³ isopycnal — the core depth of CUC influence — temperature is bimodal, separating a nearshore CUC-influenced mode (~6.75°C) from a more mixed offshore mode. Along-transect variability follows a three-part pattern: low at both the nearshore and offshore regions, but elevated in the central section, where CUC influence intermittently strengthens or weakens. Vertical sections reveal coherent mesoscale and submesoscale features — sharp fronts, surface eddies, and subsurface "cuddies" — persisting for ~10 days along the transect. Depth-integrated geostrophic transport averages 0.41 ± 0.97 Sv; both the upper (0–200 m) and intermediate (200–900 m) layers are tied to shifts in the Alaska Gyre–subtropical gyre bifurcation, though this relationship is strongest in the upper layer (77% of variance explained by sea surface height gradients from satellite altimetry) and only partly captured by altimetric gradients at depth (35%). Spectral analysis of thermal anomalies along isopycnals yields slopes near −1.5, with no clear change with depth, and linked to changes in submesoscale and mesoscale variance. Possible drivers of this slope — upwelling-favorable winds, CUC influence, the position of the Alaska Gyre–subtropical gyre bifurcation, and eddy kinetic energy — are explored, without identifying a single, conclusive mechanism.
